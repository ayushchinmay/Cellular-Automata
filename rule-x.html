Notable rules

What this is

An elementary cellular automaton. One row of cells, each on or off. To draw the next row, every cell looks at a few cells above it and asks what to become.

Rule X looks at three: its own and one either side. Three cells, on or off, gives exactly eight situations — the eight boxes. The little pattern on top is the situation, the square underneath is your answer. Eight answers read as binary make a number from 0 to 255, and that number is the rule.

Rule 2X

Four cells instead of three: sixteen situations, sixteen answers, and a rule number from 0 to 65,535. Each box still has one answer square rather than two — sixteen situations with two outputs each would mean 416, about 4.3 billion rules, and an unreadable strip.

Four cells have no middle one, so the natural home for the answer is the gap between the two centre cells. That is what stagger rows does: every other row is shifted half a cell, which puts each new cell directly under the gap it came from and keeps the pattern centred. Turn it off and the window becomes lopsided — reaching one cell left and two right — so everything slides sideways. Measured over 200 symmetric rules: half a cell of drift with stagger on, eight cells without it.

Two ways to run

By default the diagram runs forever and the board scrolls, keeping every row so you can wheel back to the seed. Tick stop at the bottom and it behaves like a sheet of paper instead: the height is whatever fits on screen, and it halts the moment the pattern reaches the last row. Change the cell size or the window and the sheet resizes with it.

Why any of it matters

Rule 30 turns a single cell into something disordered enough that its middle column passed serious randomness tests. Rule 110 was proved capable of universal computation. Both start from one black square and eight yes-or-no answers.
